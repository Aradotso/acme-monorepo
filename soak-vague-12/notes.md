# What makes a good commit message

A good commit message is brief, specific, and useful to someone reading the project history later. The subject should state the change in the imperative (for example, “Add timeout to webhook retries”), stay concise, and identify the relevant scope when that adds clarity. Keep the subject focused on one logical change rather than describing every file touched.

Use the body when context matters: explain the motivation, important implementation choices, and user-visible or operational consequences—not a line-by-line replay of the diff. Mention tests or migration steps when they affect reviewers or deployers, and reference an issue or ticket when one exists. Call out breaking changes explicitly, including what users must do, and preserve required trailers such as signed-off-by or co-author lines exactly as specified. If the change is a revert, dependency update, merge, or generated-file refresh, say so plainly and include the relevant version or original commit; do not disguise it as an ordinary feature or fix. Avoid vague claims, unrelated cleanup, secrets, and assumptions the diff cannot support. The message should remain accurate even when a change is small, documentation-only, partially automated, or later amended.
